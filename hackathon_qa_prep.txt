BLOCK PLANNER — HACKATHON Q&A PREP
=====================================
A guide to likely judge questions and honest, defensible answers.
Read this together as a team before the demo — everyone should be able to
answer the "expect this to get pushed hard" questions the same way.


SECTION 1: THE PROBLEM / DOMAIN
=====================================

Q: What real railway problem does this solve, and for whom?
A: It solves maintenance block planning — deciding which maintenance tasks
   get scheduled into which track possession windows, across multiple
   departments (Engineering, Traction, S&T/Signals), while minimizing
   train disruption. It's aimed at the planners/supervisors who currently
   coordinate this manually across departments.

Q: How is this done today without your tool, and what's the actual pain?
A: Answer honestly based on your team's research. If you don't have a
   verified answer, say: "We believe the current process is largely manual
   coordination between department planners, which creates two problems:
   (1) departments schedule blocks independently, missing opportunities to
   combine work into fewer possessions, and (2) prioritization is
   inconsistent/experience-based rather than data-driven." Don't claim
   specifics you haven't verified with a real railway stakeholder.

Q: Why 3 corridors / this specific scale — is it representative of a real
   network, or arbitrary?
A: It's a representative subset chosen to demonstrate the approach at a
   scale a judge can inspect and reason about. It is not derived from a
   specific real network's topology. The algorithm is not hardcoded to 3
   corridors — it groups tasks/windows by whatever corridor_id values
   exist in the data, so it generalizes.

Q: What's your source for the domain rules (block types, safety
   requirement conflicts, department names)?
A: Be honest here. If these were inferred/invented for the prototype
   rather than sourced from a domain expert or official documentation,
   say so directly: "These rules were our best approximation based on
   [research/publicly available info/assumptions], not validated by a
   railway safety expert. That validation would be a necessary next step
   before any real deployment."


SECTION 2: PRIORITY SCORING
=====================================

Q: Why these exact weights — 30% asset criticality, 25% failure risk,
   20% overdue factor, 15% train impact, 10% safety criticality?
A: If you have real justification (stakeholder input, research), lead
   with it. Otherwise be honest: "These are reasoned defaults reflecting
   that asset criticality and failure risk should dominate the score,
   with safety criticality weighted lower because in our model most
   safety-critical tasks already surface as high-criticality or
   high-risk anyway. Critically, the weights aren't hardcoded — they're
   stored in a priority_weights table and are meant to be tuned by
   domain experts for their specific network." Do NOT claim these came
   from real operational data unless they actually did.

Q: Why logarithmic scaling for the overdue factor, and why cap at 30 days?
A: log1p(days_overdue) / log1p(30) means the first few overdue days
   matter a lot (steep early slope) and additional overdue days matter
   progressively less — the idea being that "14 days overdue" should
   score meaningfully higher than "1 day overdue," but "60 days overdue"
   shouldn't dwarf everything else in the formula. The 30-day cap sets
   the scale of "maximally urgent" for normalization purposes; it's a
   modeling choice, not derived from a real overdue-risk curve.

Q: If I change the weights in the UI, does that actually change the
   generated plan, or just redraw a number?
A: Verify this yourself before the demo. Trace it: priority_weights →
   calculate_priority_score() → task.priority_score → does the optimizer
   re-run using updated scores, and does the frontend re-fetch and
   re-render the new plan? If any part of that chain is broken, either
   fix it or don't claim it works live.

Q: How would you validate this scoring model is actually good?
A: Honestly: "We don't have ground truth (real incident/outcome data) to
   validate against yet. In a real deployment, we'd want to backtest the
   scoring against historical incidents — does a high priority_score
   correlate with tasks that, when delayed, actually led to failures or
   safety events?" This is a legitimate and expected limitation to admit.


SECTION 3: THE OPTIMIZER (BLOCK PACKING)
=====================================
*** THIS IS THE SECTION MOST LIKELY TO GET PUSHED HARD. READ CAREFULLY. ***

IMPORTANT CONTEXT FOR THE TEAM: block_optimizer.py currently instantiates
an OR-Tools CpModel and CpSolver in __init__(), but optimize() does NOT
use them — it runs a greedy heuristic (sort by priority, pack tasks into
windows corridor-by-corridor). If your pitch materials say "constraint
programming" or "CP-SAT optimization," this is a gap a technical judge
can find by reading the code for 30 seconds. Decide as a team, before
the demo, whether to (a) actually wire up CP-SAT, or (b) adjust your
pitch language to accurately describe a greedy heuristic. Below assumes
you go with an honest answer either way.

Q: You mention OR-Tools/CP-SAT — walk me through where the constraint
   model is actually solved.
A (if not fixed by demo time): "We instantiate the CP-SAT solver as part
   of our architecture, but the current packing algorithm is a greedy
   heuristic: sort pending tasks by priority score, then for each
   corridor's block windows, greedily pack the highest-priority
   compatible tasks until the window is ~70% full. We chose greedy first
   because it's fast and predictable at this scale, with CP-SAT as the
   natural next step for finding provably better solutions, especially
   as constraints grow more complex."
A (if fixed by demo time): describe the actual CP-SAT formulation —
   variables, constraints, objective — accurately.

Q: Is your packing algorithm provably optimal, or just a reasonable
   heuristic? What guarantees does it give?
A: No optimality guarantee. It's a greedy, priority-ordered heuristic.
   It will not backtrack — once a task is assigned to a window, that
   decision is final even if a later, better combination becomes
   apparent. This is a real limitation worth stating plainly.

Q: How do you know the greedy approach doesn't leave easy wins on the
   table?
A: We haven't benchmarked it against an exact solver (e.g., running the
   same instance through CP-SAT and comparing task-completion counts or
   utilization). That comparison is a natural validation step we'd want
   to do before claiming the plan is "optimized" rather than "improved."

Q: Your compatibility check only hardcodes two conflicting safety-
   requirement pairs (Power Block Required + Lines Up, Track Isolation +
   Power Block Required). Where did those come from, and what about
   combinations you haven't hardcoded?
A: These were illustrative examples of real conflict types (you can't
   run two mutually exclusive access modes in the same block), not an
   exhaustive, expert-verified conflict matrix. A real system would need
   a domain-expert-authored compatibility matrix, not two hardcoded
   tuples.

Q: What's your algorithm's time complexity, and how does it scale?
A: For each corridor, tasks are sorted (O(n log n)) and then, for each
   window, we iterate the remaining eligible tasks once (roughly
   O(windows * tasks) per corridor in the worst case, since eligible
   tasks are filtered by not-yet-assigned each time). At 300 tasks and a
   few windows per corridor this is fast (sub-second); it has not been
   tested at national-network scale (thousands of tasks/corridors).

Q: What happens to a task that can't fit any window — a corridor with
   tasks but no compatible windows?
A: It's silently dropped from that planning cycle (the loop just
   `continue`s past corridors with no windows, and tasks that don't fit
   simply aren't selected). It remains "Pending" and should reappear in
   the next planning cycle. In a safety-critical context, silently
   dropping a task without surfacing it as "could not be scheduled" is a
   real gap — flag this as a known limitation rather than pretend the
   system handles it gracefully today.

Q: How do train conflicts actually reduce available block time?
A: If the number of conflicting trains exceeds window_duration // 30, we
   reserve 15 minutes per conflicting train off the top of the window's
   available duration before packing tasks. This is a simplification
   (real conflict resolution would need actual train movement windows,
   not just a flat per-train time penalty).


SECTION 4: DATA — IS IT REAL?
=====================================

Q: Is any of this real railway data, or synthetic?
A: It's synthetic, generated by our own data generator (data_generator.py
   / seed.py) to produce a realistic-looking but fabricated dataset of
   assets, tasks, trains, and corridors. Be upfront and immediate about
   this if asked directly — don't let a judge "catch" it.

Q: How did you validate the synthetic data is realistic?
A: Be honest about your process — e.g., "we chose distributions and
   value ranges (criticality, failure rates, durations) that felt
   plausible based on [whatever research you did], but we have not
   validated them against real operational data."

Q: What happens with messy/incomplete real-world data — missing due
   dates, conflicting dependencies, duplicate IDs?
A: Currently, minimal defensive handling — the models assume reasonably
   clean input (see app/core/validation.py for what is validated). This
   is a known gap for production readiness: real data pipelines are
   messier than generated data.

stub folders (just blank __init__.py files). If your pitch mentions

Q: Show me the actual integration code for [TMS/SMMS/TDMS/COA].
A: "These are placeholder modules representing planned integration
   points in our architecture — they are not implemented. Our current
   prototype uses generated/seeded data in place of live feeds from
   these systems." Do not imply these are functional if asked directly.

Q: What would a real integration with one of these systems actually
   require?
A: Authentication/access agreements with the system owner, agreed data
   formats (likely not REST/JSON — many railway ops systems use older

   good question to have a thoughtful answer to even without code,
   since it shows you understand deployment is harder than the demo.


SECTION 6: AUTH / SECURITY
=====================================

Q: Explain your auth flow.
A: JWT-based: /api/auth/login issues a short-lived access token (30 min
   default) and a longer-lived refresh token (7 days default), both
   HS256-signed(symmetric signing algorithm that uses HMAC combined with SHA-256 to create and verify JSON 
   Web Tokens (JWTs)). Protected endpoints use a FastAPI dependency
   (get_authenticated_user) that decodes and validates the bearer token.
   Role-based access (admin/planner/operations/engineer/viewer) is
   enforced via require_roles() dependencies on specific endpoints (e.g.
   only admins can create users).

Q: What happens to sessions when the server restarts?
A: This was a real bug we hit and fixed: JWT_SECRET_KEY defaults to a
   randomly generated value if not set in the environment, which means
   every restart invalidates all previously issued tokens (signature
   verification fails). We fixed this by pinning a stable secret in the
   environment config, so tokens now survive restarts/reloads. (Good
   story if asked "what's a real bug you hit and fixed" — this one has
   a clean before/after.)

Q: Your MOCK_USERS store is in-memory — what happens to created users on
   restart?
A: They reset to the 5 default seeded users (admin, planner, operations,
   engineer, viewer) every restart, since there's no persistent user
   store. This is acceptable for a demo but is a known limitation —
   production would need users persisted in the database, not an
   in-memory dict.

Q: Rate limiting is in-memory per-process — how does this behave with
   multiple backend instances (horizontal scaling)?
A: It doesn't share state across instances — each process has its own
   independent rate-limit counters, so real enforced limits under a
   load-balanced multi-instance deployment would need a shared store
   (e.g., Redis), not the current in-memory approach.

Q: Why sha256_crypt instead of bcrypt/argon2 for password hashing?
A: The code comment says "for better compatibility." If pushed, be
   honest that bcrypt/argon2 are generally considered the stronger
   modern choices for password hashing, and sha256_crypt was likely
   chosen to avoid a native-dependency install issue during development
   rather than for security reasons. Acknowledging this rather than
   defending it as a deliberate security choice will read better to a
   judge with security background.

Q: Where do secrets live — is the Supabase service key (write-capable)
   ever exposed client-side?
A: Verify this yourselves before the demo: SUPABASE_SERVICE_KEY should
   only ever be used server-side (backend .env), never shipped to the
   frontend bundle. The anon key is safe to expose client-side as long
   as Row Level Security policies on the Supabase tables are correctly
   locked down — confirm your RLS policies actually restrict access
   appropriately, since the anon key alone is enough to query any table
   without RLS.


SECTION 7: ARCHITECTURE / SCALE
=====================================

Q: Why Supabase/Postgres via REST (PostgREST) rather than a direct DB
   connection?
A: Faster to stand up for a hackathon timeline, gives free REST/CRUD
   generation, built-in auth/RLS primitives, and no need to manage a
   database connection pool ourselves. Tradeoff: an extra network hop
   per query versus a direct DB connection, and dependency on
   Supabase's availability/latency.

Q: Walk through what happens on a dashboard load.
A: The backend fires concurrent requests (via a thread pool) to fetch
   tasks, blocks, and assets from Supabase in parallel, then assembles
   them into a single dashboard response. We initially had these on
   separate ad-hoc HTTP connections per call; we fixed this to use a
   single shared, connection-pooled httpx.Client to avoid connection
   contention under concurrent load (this was a real timeout bug we
   diagnosed and fixed — good story if asked about debugging).

Q: Why threads instead of native async/await, given FastAPI is
   async-first?
A: Honest answer: the current data layer uses a synchronous httpx
   client called via a ThreadPoolExecutor inside sync endpoints (which
   FastAPI itself runs in its own threadpool), rather than an async
   httpx client with native async/await. This works but isn't the
   idiomatic FastAPI pattern — a fully async rewrite (async
   httpx.AsyncClient, async endpoint functions) would be more scalable
   and is a reasonable improvement to name if asked about future work.

Q: How would this scale to a full national network (thousands of assets,
   corridors)?
A: The greedy optimizer's complexity and the synchronous per-request
   Supabase calls are the two components most likely to need rework —
   the optimizer for algorithmic scale, and the data layer for
   concurrency/caching. Neither has been load-tested beyond the ~300
   task demo dataset.

Q: What's a real bug you hit while building this?
A: Tell the Supabase concurrent-timeout story and the JWT-secret-
   regeneration story — both are genuine, diagnosed-and-fixed bugs with
   clear before/after explanations (see Sections 6 and 7 above). These
   make for much stronger answers than a rehearsed generic one, because
   you can actually explain the root cause.


SECTION 8: EXPLAINABILITY / "AI" CLAIMS
=====================================

Q: You call this "explainable AI" / "AI-driven prioritization" — walk me
   through the model.
A: Be precise here — this is a common judge trap. There is no trained
   machine learning model in this system. Priority scores come from a
   fixed weighted formula (Section 2) over input fields, and the
   "explanation" text is a template that fills in which of those fields
   crossed a threshold (e.g., "High failure risk: 90.9%" if
   failure_risk >= 70). This is a rule-based, transparent scoring
   system — which is a legitimate and often preferable design for a
   safety-critical domain (auditable, deterministic, no black box) — but
   it is not machine learning, and you should not describe it as such if
   directly asked whether there's a trained model involved.

Q: Is there any actual ML anywhere (failure prediction, anomaly
   detection)?
A: If none exists yet: say so plainly, and note that failure_risk is
   currently an input field in the seeded/synthetic data rather than a
   predicted value — a real predictive-maintenance model (e.g., trained
   on sensor/inspection history to estimate failure_risk) would be a
   natural and valuable extension, but isn't built.


SECTION 9: PRACTICAL / DEMO-DAY
=====================================

Q: What's the actual novel contribution here versus existing scheduling
   / CMMS (Computerized Maintenance Management System) tools?
A: Prepare this as a team — be specific about what's different: is it
   the multi-department coordination into shared blocks? The priority
   transparency? The what-if simulation? Don't answer vaguely with
   "it's all-in-one" — name the 1-2 things that are actually distinct.

Q: What did you not have time to build, and what's your roadmap?
A: Have an honest, prioritized list ready — e.g., real CP-SAT
   optimization, real system integrations, ML-based failure prediction,
   persistent user management, load testing at scale. Judges respond
   well to a clear-eyed roadmap more than to claims that everything is
   finished.

Q: If something breaks live on stage (e.g., a timeout or auth error),
   what's your fallback?
A: Decide and rehearse this as a team before demo day: do you have
   cached/pre-loaded data you can fall back to, a graceful error state
   in the UI rather than a blank screen, or a recorded backup demo video
   as a last resort? Given you've already hit both a blank-screen crash
   bug and an auth-401 bug once each, treat "what if it happens again on
   stage" as a real risk to plan for, not a hypothetical.


SECTION 10: QUICK-REFERENCE — KNOWN GAPS TO BE UPFRONT ABOUT
=====================================
(If a judge finds these by reading code, "yes, we know, here's why /
here's the plan" lands far better than getting caught off guard.)

1. block_optimizer.py sets up OR-Tools CP-SAT but never uses it — actual
   packing is a greedy heuristic.
2. app/integrations/{coa,smms,tdms,tms} are empty stub folders — no real
   system integrations exist.
3. All data is synthetic/generated, not real railway operational data.
4. MOCK_USERS is in-memory and resets to 5 default users on every
   restart.
5. Rate limiting is in-memory per-process, won't work correctly across
   multiple scaled instances.
6. Priority "explanations" are template text based on thresholds, not
   output from a trained model — there is no ML in the system currently.
7. Password hashing uses sha256_crypt rather than bcrypt/argon2.
8. The optimizer silently drops tasks that don't fit any window rather
   than surfacing them as "could not schedule."
